Menopause brings abrupt changes in bone, liver and blood fats, huge study finds

Menopause is usually described as a slow fade. A study of more than a million women suggests something different: a sharp step change across the body at the final period, with hormone shifts starting more than a decade before.

By the Parva Regina Research Desk · Checked against sources October 28, 2026 · How we work

Quick answer. Researchers analyzed over 300 million routine lab tests from more than a million women in the US and Israel. Many markers, especially bone, liver and blood-fat tests, shifted abruptly around the final menstrual period, while key hormones began changing 12 to 15 years earlier. It’s observational, so it shows when things change, not what to do about it.
Human observational  Evidence at a glance
Study type
Retrospective analysis of routine lab records (observational)
Who
More than 1 million women in the United States and Israel, 1999–2024
Published
Nature Medicine, online October 12, 2026
Bottom line
Strong evidence about timing; no evidence yet about which interventions change the curve

What the menopause study found

The team, whose lead author is at the Weizmann Institute of Science in Israel, pooled more than 300 million laboratory tests collected during ordinary medical care. Because the final menstrual period is rarely recorded, past studies have struggled to pin down exactly when changes happen. Working with a dataset this large let them line up women by the timing of their last period and look for patterns.

  • An abrupt shift at the final period. In the US data, 52 of the 90 lab tests studied showed a statistically significant, sudden change around menopause.
  • Which systems: the biggest shifts were in bone metabolism, liver tests and how the body handles blood fats. Kidney function and blood chemistry shifted too.
  • Not just aging: men of the same ages didn’t show these abrupt changes, so the authors tie them to menopause itself rather than getting older.

Hormones start changing more than a decade earlier

For women in their late 30s and 40s, this is the part worth noticing. The hormone shifts that lead up to menopause began long before the last period:

HormoneWhat happenedWhen
Anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH)Declined rapidlyAbout 15 years before the final period
Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH)Began rising more rapidlyAbout 14 years before
EstradiolDeclined rapidlyAbout 12 years before

In other words, the menopause transition is a story that often starts in your late 30s, even if nothing feels different yet.

What about hormone therapy?

According to the journal’s press release, hormone therapy was associated with smaller changes in many measures, particularly bone, liver and kidney markers. The authors themselves say this needs to be confirmed in future studies. Because women weren’t randomly assigned to hormone therapy here, the difference could reflect who chooses it, not just what it does.

What the study can’t tell you

  • It’s observational. It shows when lab values change, not what causes the change or what prevents it.
  • Menopause status was self-reported, and the data were collected for routine care, not research.
  • “Cliff” describes lab curves, not how women feel. Experts not involved in the study cautioned that the framing could sound more alarming than the findings, which don’t show a dramatic physical or mental decline.

What this means for you

Nothing here calls for a new supplement or test. What it does support is paying attention before menopause, not after:

  • Ask your clinician how they’d monitor bone health and cholesterol as you move through your 40s, and what your personal risk factors are.
  • Keep building muscle and bone with strength training, which has the strongest evidence of any habit for both.
  • If you’re weighing hormone therapy, that’s a conversation about your own risks and benefits with your doctor, not a decision to make from one observational study.
