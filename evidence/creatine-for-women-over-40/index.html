Creatine for women over 40: what the research actually shows

Creatine has gone from gym-bro powder to midlife-women’s staple in about two years. The pitch: more muscle, stronger bones, sharper thinking. Here’s what the trials in women actually found.

By the Parva Regina Research Desk · Checked against sources October 28, 2026 · How we work

Quick answer. In postmenopausal women, the best trials show creatine adds little or nothing to bone density and only modest extra strength beyond what resistance training already gives. Training is the main event; creatine is, at most, a small add-on. Brain and mood claims rest on small, short studies.
Human trial  Evidence at a glance
Best evidence
A 2-year randomized, placebo-controlled trial in 237 postmenopausal women (2023)
Women included?
Yes. The main trials studied women only, mostly after menopause
Dose tested
0.14 g per kg of body weight daily in the 2-year trial, alongside resistance training
Bottom line
No bone-density benefit; small, inconsistent muscle and strength effects; brain findings are early

Why creatine for women over 40 is suddenly everywhere

From the mid-30s on, keeping muscle and bone becomes a priority, and the menopause transition brings an abrupt shift in bone metabolism (see our news report on a study of a million women). Creatine, a compound your muscles already use for short bursts of energy, has strong evidence for helping athletes train harder. The question is whether that carries over to women in midlife who want to keep their strength and their skeleton.

That’s a fair question, and it has been tested. Not in mice, but in randomized trials of women.

What the biggest trial found: creatine and bone density

The largest and longest study so far followed 237 postmenopausal women, average age 59, for two years. Every woman did the same program: resistance training three days a week and walking six days a week. Half took creatine monohydrate (0.14 g per kilogram of body weight a day); half took a placebo. Neither the women nor the researchers knew who got which.

The results, published in 2023:

  • Bone density: no difference. Bone mineral density at the hip (femoral neck), the study’s main measure, changed about the same in both groups.
  • Bone shape: the creatine group held on to one measure of hip bone geometry (section modulus) that dropped slightly in the placebo group. That’s a hint about bone strength, not proof of fewer fractures.
  • Strength: no difference. Both groups got much stronger on the bench press and squat, because both groups were training.
  • Lean mass: a small edge for creatine only among women who stuck closely to the program; in the full group, it wasn’t significant.
  • Walking speed: the creatine group got slightly faster over an 80-meter walk.

Notice what the trial design tells you: everyone trained, and everyone got stronger. Strength training itself is the better-studied tool for muscle and bone.

Does creatine build muscle in women after menopause?

A 2026 review pooled trials that combined supplements with exercise in women across reproductive stages. It included three creatine trials, all in postmenopausal women, with about 300 women analyzed. Its findings:

  • Upper-body strength improved a little more when creatine was added, which the authors attribute largely to creatine.
  • Muscle mass results were mixed: one short trial found a gain, the two longer ones didn’t.
  • Exercise alone was the most reliable intervention. The authors rated the certainty of the evidence as low for muscle and strength and very low for bone.

What about brain fog and mood?

This is the newest and thinnest part of the evidence. One randomized trial of 36 perimenopausal and menopausal women (average age 50) tested creatine hydrochloride, a different form from the usual monohydrate, for 8 weeks. The middle dose (1,500 mg a day) raised creatine levels in the front of the brain and changed reaction time compared with placebo. The effect on mood swings didn’t reach statistical significance.

That’s interesting, and it’s also 36 women for two months. It’s a reason for bigger studies, not a reason to expect clearer thinking.

To put the big trial’s dose in context: 0.14 g per kilogram works out to roughly 9.5 g a day for a 150-lb (68 kg) woman. That’s the amount a study tested, not a recommendation.

The honest bottom line

  • If your goal is bone: creatine hasn’t shown a bone-density benefit in women after menopause. Strength training and a conversation with your clinician about bone screening are the better-supported moves.
  • If your goal is strength: the training does the heavy lifting. Creatine may add a little on top, especially for the upper body.
  • If your goal is your brain: it’s too early to say.

Creatine is one of the most-studied supplements in sport, but most of that research is in young men. Talk to your clinician before starting it, especially if you have kidney disease, take medication that affects your kidneys, or are pregnant or nursing.
